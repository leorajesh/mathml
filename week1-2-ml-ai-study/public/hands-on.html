Act 1 · 10 minutes

Teach a machine to decide

Each dot is a student: how many hours they studied and how many hours they slept before the exam. Blue passed, orange did not. Press Train and watch the machine find a line that separates them.

The machine starts knowing nothing. It visits the students one by one, and every time it gets one wrong it nudges three numbers a little. When a whole pass goes by with no mistakes, it stops. That is the whole of learning here.

What the chat box hides

An AI model is a set of numbers adjusted until its mistakes on examples become rare. It does not know what "studying" is. It knows three numbers that happen to work on these dots.

At frontier scale

This model has 3 numbers. A frontier language model has hundreds of billions, adjusted the same way: guess, measure the error, nudge.

From SUTD: the perceptron and linear classifiers (Production Ready ML), vectors and dot products (Mathematics for AI).

Presenter notes · Act 1
  • Press Train once and narrate: "wrong, nudge… wrong, nudge… now a pass with no mistakes."
  • Invite someone to click a dot where it clearly does not belong (a blue dot deep in the orange area), then Train again. The line moves, and may never settle: no straight line fits, so it keeps adjusting.
  • Point at the formula readout: "That is the entire 'brain'. Three numbers."

Act 2 · 10 minutes

Build your own autocomplete

Our tiny language model reads the text on the right and counts which word tends to come next. To write, it looks at the last few words, picks a likely next word, and repeats.

Type the library opens at. The text says the library opens at nine. Yet the model often answers ten or eight, fluently and without hesitation, because with a short memory it only sees "opens at", and in this text the cafe and the hall also open at something.

What the chat box hides

A language model predicts likely words. Sounding confident and being correct are different things; a fluent wrong answer is exactly what this process can produce. This is where "hallucinations" come from.

At frontier scale

Frontier models read far more text and remember far more context, and a lot of extra training makes them helpful and more reliable. The core step is the same: score every possible next word, pick one, repeat.

From SUTD: probability and likelihood (Mathematics for AI), logistic outputs as probabilities (Production Ready ML).

Study it in the portal: Likelihood Probabilities from scores Markov chains

Presenter notes · Act 2

  • With memory 2, click Show next-word odds for "the library opens at": about 1/3 each for nine, ten and eight. Then set memory to 3: now it is always nine, because it copies the text more closely.
  • Ask the room to paste in their own paragraph (a school notice works well) and generate from it.
  • Turn up creativity: more surprising and more wrong. Turn it down: more repetitive.

Act 3 · 8 minutes

Memorise or learn?

Ten practice points (filled) follow a smooth pattern plus some noise. The slider sets how flexible the model's curve is allowed to be.

Drag it to the right. The curve bends to pass through every practice point and its practice error falls to almost zero. Then reveal the new data (hollow points) it never saw: the flexible curve is badly wrong on them. It memorised the noise instead of learning the pattern.

What the chat box hides

"It got 100% on the benchmark" can mean it memorised the benchmark. What matters is performance on questions it has never seen, which is why careful teams keep test data locked away until the very end.

At frontier scale

When a model has seen the test questions during training, its scores look better than its real ability. This "contamination" is a live concern in how AI models are compared.

From SUTD: polynomial regression, overfitting, train/validation/test splits (Production Ready ML), least squares (Mathematics for AI).

Study it in the portal: Polynomial regression Overfitting Train, validation, test

Act 4 · 7 minutes

Whose data taught it?

A plant-disease detector looks at leaf colour and spot size. It was trained on photos from Farm A. Farm B photographs its leaves in sunlight, so its leaves look greener and the signs of disease look different.

With no Farm B photos in training, the detector is excellent on Farm A and poor on Farm B, and nothing in its output tells you so. Slide in more Farm B photos and watch its accuracy on Farm B recover.

What the chat box hides

A model's accuracy is an average over the people and cases it was tested on. Groups that are missing from the data can get much worse results, even when the overall number looks excellent.

In the real world

The Gender Shades study (Buolamwini and Gebru, 2018) found commercial face-analysis systems were far less accurate for darker-skinned women than for lighter-skinned men, largely because of who was in the data.

From SUTD: feature engineering, missing data and bias, distribution shift and monitoring in production (Production Ready ML).

Study it in the portal: Missing data and bias ML in production Classification metrics

Presenter notes · Act 4
  • Start at 0% Farm B. Read out both accuracies (about 99% and 74%). Ask: "Would you deploy this at Farm B?" Point out the averaged accuracy still looks fine.
  • Slide to 10%, then 20%. Farm B jumps from about 74% to 97% while Farm A stays at 99%. The fix was data, not a smarter algorithm.
  • Bridge to people: "Replace farms with accents, age groups or neighbourhoods."

Take-aways · 5 minutes

From user to informed judge

You do not need to build models to use AI wisely. But a little of what is inside changes the questions you ask.

SituationA user asksSomeone who understands also asks
A chatbot answers a factual questionIs this answer good?Could this be a fluent guess? Where can I check it? (Act 2)
A tool claims 99% accuracyGreat, let's use it.99% on what data, and was that data kept separate from training? (Act 3)
A system decides about peopleDoes it work?Does it work equally well for everyone, including groups missing from its data? (Act 4)
The model was trained last yearIt worked before.Has the world changed since? Who is watching for that? (production ML)

Five questions to ask any AI tool

  1. What was it trained on?And who or what is missing from that data?
  2. How was it tested?On data it had never seen, from people like the ones it will serve?
  3. What does it do when unsure?Does it say so, or answer just as confidently?
  4. What happens when things change?New users, new rules, a new year. Who monitors it?
  5. Who checks its mistakes?How would we even notice one, and who can fix it?
